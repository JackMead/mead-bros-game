# Puzzle design: what we borrow from the classics

The rubric says what every puzzle must be. This note is about how to make a chapter genuinely hard without breaking those rules. It draws on the LucasArts games of the late 80s and 90s, especially the Monkey Island series, and on Ron Gilbert's own writing ("Why Adventure Games Suck" and his puzzle dependency charts). Examples here are invented or from those games, never from our chapters.

## 1. Branch wide, then converge

Draw the chapter as a dependency chart: each puzzle a box, arrows to what it unlocks. A chart that is one long line is easy however many boxes it has, because there is only ever one thing to do next.

The good shape is a fan: one goal that needs three or four things, each at the end of its own short chain, all open at the same time. Monkey Island 2's middle section works like this. The player always has several threads going, and doesn't know which item or clue belongs to which thread.

- Aim for at least one point in the chapter where three or more threads are open at once.
- Let the threads cross: an item from one thread is the key to another.

## 2. Items with more than one plausible use

If each item fits exactly one place, the puzzle is just finding that place. Better:

- **An item that looks right in three places and is right in one.** A rope could tie the boat, climb the wall or lead the goat.
- **An item used twice**, for different jobs, so using it once isn't the end of it.
- **An item you need back.** You lend it to one thread and have to recover it for another. Make sure it can always be recovered (no dead ends).

## 3. Knowledge as the key

Monkey Island 1's sword fighting is won with insults and comebacks you pick up around the island, not with an item. Answers you remember or work out resist brute force, because trying every item on everything doesn't help.

- Spread the knowledge across scenes and characters.
- Make the moment of use a deliberate action that only makes sense if you know.

## 4. Don't state the need

"Bring me a fish" makes the puzzle "find a fish". Better: show a character's problem and let the player work out what would solve it. A guard who keeps nodding off; a gate that won't stay shut. Use refusals to narrow it down, never to name the answer.

## 5. Red herrings: rare, funny, and fair

One or two things per chapter that look important and aren't. Each needs a funny refusal everywhere it's tried, and nothing should point at it more strongly than at the real answer.

## 6. Sideways but fair

The best answers make you groan "of course". The answer sits in plain sight, used in a way nobody picks first. It should make sense the moment you see it.

What not to copy: answers that rely on a pun, an obscure fact the game never tells you, or a line of reasoning only the designer could follow. Monkey Island 2's monkey wrench is the famous cautionary example. If the answer needs a pun, the game must spell out the pun first.

## 7. Character puzzles

A brother's trait that both creates the obstacle and solves it is our best tool. Players remember those puzzles, and they're hard to brute force because the answer comes from who the brother is.
